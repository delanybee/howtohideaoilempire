The Pipeline Network

The United States operates the most extensive pipeline network in the world: 85,000 miles of crude oil lines and over 200,000 miles total when including refined products and natural gas liquids.

2.8M total pipeline miles (all types)
72B barrels transported annually

Source: PHMSA, Bureau of Transportation Statistics, American Petroleum Institute

Permian Basin to Gulf Coast

The Permian Basin in West Texas is America's most productive oil field, producing over 6 million barrels per day, nearly half of total U.S. output. Multiple major pipelines carry crude to Gulf Coast refineries and export terminals:

  • Cactus Pipeline: 670,000 bpd capacity, 660 miles
  • EPIC Pipeline: 600,000 bpd capacity, 730 miles
  • Gray Oak Pipeline: 900,000 bpd capacity, 900 miles
  • Permian Highway: 2.1 Bcf/d natural gas capacity
  • Wink to Webster: 1M bpd capacity, 650 miles

Combined Permian takeaway capacity exceeds 4.5 million bpd of crude oil.

The Cushing Hub

Cushing, Oklahoma serves as the "Pipeline Crossroads of the World." Oil from Canadian oil sands and the Bakken formation flows here through massive networks:

  • Plains All American: 14,919 miles of pipeline
  • Enbridge: 12,974 miles of pipeline
  • Keystone Pipeline: 2,687 miles from Alberta to Gulf Coast
  • Seaway Pipeline: 500 miles, reversed in 2012 to flow south

Cushing's tank farms hold up to 90 million barrels, and WTI prices are benchmarked to deliveries here.

Colonial Pipeline

The Colonial Pipeline runs from Houston to New York Harbor, carrying 2.5 million barrels per day of refined products. It supplies nearly half of the East Coast's fuel.

The 2021 ransomware attack demonstrated just how dependent the East Coast is on this single piece of infrastructure.

California: The Fuel Island

Unlike every other major U.S. market, California has no pipeline connection to other refining regions. The state must rely entirely on:

  • In-state production and refining
  • Marine imports from foreign sources

This isolation makes California vulnerable to supply disruptions and price spikes.

Offshore Pipeline Networks

Beyond onshore systems, 8,600+ miles of active offshore pipelines connect platforms to shore in the Gulf of Mexico alone.

  • Mars Pipeline: 220 miles, 600,000 bpd capacity
  • Caesar Pipeline: 215 miles deepwater system
  • Poseidon Pipeline: 100 miles, connects major deepwater fields

Subsea pipelines rest on the ocean floor at depths up to 7,000+ feet.

U.S. Offshore Oil Production

Offshore Oil Platforms

The U.S. operates hundreds of offshore platforms in federal waters, concentrated primarily in the Gulf of Mexico. These structures range from shallow-water fixed platforms to deepwater floating systems in water over 10,000 feet deep.

Gulf of Mexico: America's Offshore Heartland

1,862
active platforms in the Gulf

The Gulf of Mexico accounts for 97% of all U.S. offshore oil production and 15% of total U.S. crude output.

  • Production: ~1.8 million barrels per day
  • Proven reserves: 4.7 billion barrels
  • Active leases: 2,700+ covering 14.7 million acres

Source: BSEE, EIA

Deepwater Frontier

Deepwater (1,000+ feet) and ultra-deepwater (5,000+ feet) production now accounts for the majority of Gulf output:

  • Thunder Horse: BP, 250,000 bpd, 6,050 ft depth
  • Atlantis: BP, 200,000 bpd, 7,070 ft depth
  • Mars/Ursa: Shell, 230,000 bpd combined
  • Perdido: Shell, world's deepest at 9,627 ft
  • Mad Dog: BP, 140,000 bpd, expanded in 2023

Deepwater wells can cost $100-200 million each to drill.

Major Operators

A handful of companies dominate Gulf production:

  • Shell: ~400,000 bpd, largest Gulf producer
  • BP: ~300,000 bpd, major deepwater presence
  • Chevron: ~250,000 bpd, Anchor project at record depth
  • ExxonMobil: ~150,000 bpd
  • Murphy Oil, LLOG, W&T: Significant independent operators

The top 5 operators control over 75% of Gulf production.

Platform Infrastructure

Offshore platforms are engineering marvels requiring massive support systems:

  • Fixed platforms: 1,700+ structures in shallow water
  • Floating systems: 50+ FPSOs, spars, and TLPs
  • Subsea completions: 3,500+ wells tied back to host platforms
  • Offshore pipelines: 8,600+ miles of active lines
  • Helicopter flights: 600,000+ annually for crew transport

Pacific OCS: A Different Story

Unlike the Gulf, Pacific offshore drilling is constrained:

1,862 Gulf platforms
vs.
23 Pacific platforms

Federal moratoria banned new Pacific leasing from 1984 to 2008, and California state waters have been closed since 1969. Existing platforms operate on legacy leases.

Source: BSEE Pacific OCS Region

Alaska OCS

Alaska's offshore potential remains largely untapped:

  • Beaufort Sea: 2 active production islands (Endicott, Northstar)
  • Cook Inlet: 15 platforms, declining production
  • Chukchi Sea: Exploration halted, no production
  • Estimated reserves: 23.6 billion barrels technically recoverable

Harsh conditions, environmental concerns, and high costs limit Arctic development.

Pipeline Safety: The Daily Reality

Despite industry safety claims, pipeline incidents remain a near-daily occurrence across the United States.

The Numbers

9,329
total incidents from 2010 to 2024

That averages to 628 incidents per year, or roughly 1.7 incidents every single day.

Source: PHMSA Pipeline Incident 20 Year Trends, FracTracker Alliance analysis (April 2025)

2024 Snapshot

In 2024 alone, there were 530+ reported incidents. "Significant" incidents include those involving:

  • Fatalities or hospitalizations
  • Releases of 5+ barrels of hazardous liquid
  • Property damage exceeding $50,000

Average property damage per hazardous liquid accident: $947,700

Texas: The Epicenter

Texas accounts for a disproportionate share of pipeline incidents:

30% of all U.S. incidents
vs.
11.3% of pipeline miles

2,881 incidents in Texas since 2010, out of 9,329 total nationwide.

Texas vs. National Rate

When normalized by pipeline mileage, Texas experiences incidents at nearly 3x the national average rate.

Possible factors include aging infrastructure, high throughput, and regulatory environment.

Sources: PHMSA Pipeline Incident 20 Year Trends, FracTracker Alliance

Major U.S. Oil Storage Hubs

Tank Farms and Storage

Oil storage facilities are critical nodes in the petroleum supply chain, holding crude oil between extraction and refining, and refined products before distribution.

Cushing, Oklahoma

Cushing serves as the delivery point for West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil futures, making it the pricing benchmark for American oil.

Nederland Terminal, Texas

The largest single-owned crude terminal in the United States, connected to multiple pipeline systems and the Port Arthur refinery complex.

LOOP/Clovelly, Louisiana

40M

The Louisiana Offshore Oil Port (LOOP) is the only U.S. port capable of handling Ultra Large Crude Carriers. Clovelly's salt cavern storage serves as the primary gateway for imported crude.

Houston Ship Channel

16M+
barrels at Houston Fuel Oil Terminal

The Houston Ship Channel hosts multiple major terminals serving the largest concentration of refineries in the Western Hemisphere.

Enbridge Ingleside Energy Center

17.6M
barrels

The largest crude oil export terminal in the United States, handling approximately 25% of all Gulf Coast crude exports.

Strategic Petroleum Reserve

714M
barrel authorized capacity

The world's largest emergency oil supply, stored in salt caverns at 4 sites along the Texas and Louisiana Gulf Coast. Created after the 1973 oil embargo to buffer against supply disruptions.

California: A Fossil Fuel Paradox

Despite its aggressive climate policies, California remains deeply dependent on fossil fuel infrastructure. The state is both a major oil producer and a significant importer.

Offshore Platforms

27 platforms operate in waters from Huntington Beach to Point Conception:

  • 23 federal waters platforms
  • 4 state waters platforms
  • 8 platforms no longer producing, awaiting decommissioning

ExxonMobil/Sable Offshore: Platforms Harmony, Heritage, and Hondo restarted in May 2025 after a 10-year shutdown following the 2015 Refugio spill.

Platform Operators

  • Sable Offshore: Harmony, Heritage, Hondo
  • Freeport McMoRan: Harvest, Hermosa, Hidalgo, Irene
  • DCOR: Platforms A, B, C, Edith, Gilda, Gina, Habitat, Henry, Hillhouse

Kern County: California's Oil Powerhouse

80-90% of California's onshore drilling occurs in Kern County. Four of the nation's top 7 producing fields are located here:

Major Kern County Oil Fields

  • Midway-Sunset: Largest CA field, ~4 billion barrels cumulative, 27 billion barrels oil in place
  • South Belridge: 2+ billion barrels cumulative, 6,253 active wells
  • Kern River: 2 billion barrels cumulative, 9,183 active wells (Chevron)
  • Elk Hills: 2+ billion BOE, ~75 square miles

Heavy oil extraction requires steam injection, consuming massive amounts of water from the State Water Project.

California Refineries

Down from ~50 in 1980 to 9 active fuel-producing facilities as of January 2026:

Bay Area:

  • Chevron Richmond: 245K bpd
  • PBF Martinez: 157K bpd
  • Valero Benicia: 145K bpd (closing April 2026)

Los Angeles:

  • Marathon Carson: 363K bpd
  • Chevron El Segundo: 269K bpd
  • PBF Torrance: 155K bpd
  • Phillips 66 Wilmington: 139K bpd (closing 2025)

Supply Gap

1.5M bpd refining capacity
vs.
1.78M bpd demand

The gap is filled by imports from Iraq, Ecuador, and Saudi Arabia. 5 companies control 98% of California's gasoline refining capacity.

Sources: CA Energy Commission, CARB

California Terminals

  • Zenith Long Beach
  • Zenith Dominguez Hills: 4 million barrel capacity

These facilities handle both crude imports and refined product distribution.

What the Data Reveals

Infrastructure Lock-in

The scale of existing fossil fuel infrastructure, over 2.8 million miles of pipeline based on decades of investment, creates powerful economic and political incentives to maintain the status quo. Transition timelines must account for this physical reality.

Environmental and Safety Costs

With 1.7 pipeline incidents per day on average, the operational costs of this infrastructure extend beyond economics. Communities along pipeline corridors bear disproportionate risk, particularly in Texas where incident rates far exceed the national average.

California's Paradox

California demonstrates the tension between climate ambition and energy reality. The state produces significant oil, operates 27 offshore platforms, and depends on foreign imports to meet demand, all while pursuing the nation's most aggressive decarbonization policies.

The Managed Transition

The data suggests that energy transition is not a simple switch but a complex, decades-long process of infrastructure evolution. Understanding the existing system is essential for planning realistic pathways forward.

The infrastructure documented here represents neither villain nor victim. It is simply what we built. What we build next is still being decided.
